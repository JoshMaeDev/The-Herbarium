Swiss Cheese Plant  ·  Split-Leaf Philodendron

star star star star star 4.2 (1,834 growers)

The Monstera deliciosa — colloquially the Swiss Cheese Plant — is a flowering vine from the tropical forests of southern Mexico and Panama. Celebrated for its dramatic, naturally fenestrated leaves, it has become one of the most recognisable houseplants in the world. In the wild it climbs trees using aerial roots; in cultivation it rewards minimal effort with maximum spectacle.

The Deep Dive

Care science, explained.

We go beyond checklists. Each section below details the science behind what Monstera deliciosa needs — and why — so you can adapt care to your specific conditions.

Botanist's note

"The fenestrations aren't damage — they're adaptations that let wind pass through large leaves in their native tropical storms."

— Placeholder, Lead Botanist

Monstera deliciosa is susceptible to root rot when overwatered — the most common cause of decline in cultivation. The hemiepiphytic root system evolved to dry between rainfall events in its native habitat, making the "soak and dry" method ideal. Insert a finger 5 cm into the substrate; water only when dry at that depth. Terracotta pots accelerate drying and are recommended for beginners. In winter, reduce watering frequency by roughly 50% as growth slows.

Overwatering risk: High Root sensitivity: Medium

Bright, indirect light for 6–8 hours per day produces the largest, most fenestrated leaves. Direct midday sun bleaches the deep green foliage and causes scorch. North-facing windows are usually insufficient; east or west aspects with diffused light are optimal in the UK. Notably, insufficient light suppresses fenestration — younger plants may produce entire (un-split) leaves regardless of age if kept too dark. A grow light of 1000–3000 lux placed 30 cm above the canopy corrects this effectively.

Optimal: 1,000–3,000 lux Avoid direct sun

A recommended mix: 40% high-quality potting compost, 30% perlite, 20% orchid bark chips, 10% activated charcoal. This replicates the loose, organic, fast-draining epiphytic substrate of the forest floor. The target pH is 5.5–7.0 — slightly acidic to neutral. Avoid dense, peat-heavy mixes that compact over time and suffocate roots. Repot when you see roots escaping drainage holes or when the plant dries out within 24 hours of watering.

Feed monthly during the growing season (April – September) with a balanced liquid fertiliser at half the recommended strength. A higher nitrogen ratio (e.g. 3-1-2 NPK) promotes lush foliage growth. Cease fertilising entirely from October through February to prevent salt build-up in the dormant root zone. Flush the substrate with plain water every 2–3 months to prevent mineral accumulation regardless of season.

Yellow leaves most often indicate overwatering or nutrient deficiency — check roots for rot before amending the feeding schedule. Brown leaf tips suggest low humidity or fluoride sensitivity; switching to rainwater resolves most tip-burn cases. Common pest vectors include spider mites (fine webbing on leaf undersides, thriving in dry conditions), mealybugs (white cottony masses at leaf axils), and thrips (silvery streaking). Treat early with neem oil or insecticidal soap.

Spider mites Mealybugs Thrips Root rot

The most reliable method is stem-tip cuttings with at least one node and one aerial root. Place the cutting in water or moist sphagnum moss until white root tips extend 3–5 cm, then transfer to substrate. Success rate is very high (85–95%) in spring and summer. Air-layering — wrapping a node in damp sphagnum and black plastic wrap while still attached to the mother plant — achieves near-100% success and is recommended for rare cultivars. Seed propagation is possible but extremely slow and uncommon in cultivation.
